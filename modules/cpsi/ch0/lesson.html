1

The multiplication rule (principe fondamental de l'analyse combinatoire, PFAC)

↑ contents
In plain words

Counting combinatorics (analyse combinatoire) means counting how many possibilities there are, without writing them all out.

The basic tool: if you make several choices one after the other, multiply the number of options of each choice. 2 shirts and 3 trousers make 2 × 3 = 6 outfits.

  • Experiment (expérience)Any action with several possible results, like throwing a coin.a die has 6 results
  • Cartesian product A × B (produit cartésien)The set of all pairs (x, y) with x taken from A and y from B.{P, F} × {1, …, 6} has 12 pairs
  • Cardinal |A| (cardinal, card(A))The number of elements of a set A.|{P, F}| = 2
  • PFACIf step 1 has n₁ results, step 2 has n₂, …, step k has n_k, then all k steps together have n₁ × n₂ × … × n_k results. Same thing in set language: |A × B| = |A| × |B|.
Worked example
  • Example 1 (course). Throw a coin (results P = heads, F = tails, so n₁ = 2) and a die (results 1 to 6, so n₂ = 6).
  • Number of results of both together: n₁ × n₂ = 2 × 6 = 12: (P,1), (P,2), …, (P,6), (F,1), …, (F,6).
  • Example 2 (course). A class committee needs a delegate and a deputy. 5 students run for delegate D = {d₁, …, d₅}, 6 other students run for deputy A = {a₁, …, a₆}.
  • A committee is a pair (delegate, deputy), an element of D × A. Number of committees: |D × A| = |D| × |A| = 5 × 6 = 30.

Arrangements: pick p of n, order matters (arrangements)

↑ contents
In plain words

You choose p objects out of n and put them in ordered places (1st, 2nd, …). Changing the order gives a different arrangement.

Without repetition, an object used once is gone: the choices go n, n−1, n−2, … With repetition, every place has all n choices again.

  • Order matters(a, b) and (b, a) count as two different results.a podium, a code, a plate number
  • Without repetition (sans répétition, sans remise)Each object can be used only once (we do not put it back).
  • With repetition (avec répétition, avec remise)An object can be used again (we put it back).
  • n! (n factorial) (factorielle)n × (n−1) × … × 2 × 1, the product of all whole numbers from n down to 1. By convention 0! = 1.4! = 24
  • A(n, p) (A_n^p)Arrangements without repetition: n × (n−1) × … × (n−p+1) = n! / (n−p)!. Just multiply p numbers going down from n.A(15, 3) = 15 × 14 × 13
  • Ã(n, p) = n^p (arrangement avec répétition)Arrangements with repetition: n choices for each of the p places.26³
Worked example
  • Example 1 (course). E = {a, b, c}, p = 2, without repetition: (a,b), (b,a), (a,c), (c,a), (b,c), (c,b). That is A(3, 2) = 3 × 2 = 6.
  • With repetition, add (a,a), (b,b), (c,c): 3² = 9.
  • Example 2 (course). 15 candidates numbered 1 to 15 sit an exam. How many different rankings of the first 3? Order matters (1st ≠ 2nd), nobody can be placed twice.
  • A(15, 3) = 15 × 14 × 13 = 15! / 12! = 2730.
  • Example 3 (course). A plate number made of 5 distinct digits (10 digits 0 to 9 available): A(10, 5) = 10 × 9 × 8 × 7 × 6 = 30 240.
  • Example 4 (course). Words of 3 letters (meaningful or not) from a 26-letter alphabet; a letter may come back: 26 × 26 × 26 = 26³ = 17 576.

Permutations: put all n objects in a row (permutations)

↑ contents
In plain words

A permutation is an arrangement that uses all the objects: you only decide the order. It is A(n, n) = n!.

If some objects are identical (two red balls look the same), swapping them does not make a new row, so you divide by the number of ways to shuffle each group of identical objects.

  • Permutation (permutation sans répétition)An ordering of n different objects. Count: P_n = n!.{1, 2, 3} → 6 orders
  • Permutation with repetition (permutation avec répétition)An ordering of n objects split into k groups of identical objects, of sizes n₁, …, n_k (n₁ + … + n_k = n). Count: n! / (n₁! × n₂! × … × n_k!).R R B B B → 5!/(2! 3!) = 10
Worked example
  • Example 1 (course). Orders of {1, 2, 3}: 123, 132, 213, 231, 312, 321, so P₃ = 3! = 3 × 2 × 1 = 6.
  • Example 2 (course). 5 people sit on a bench of 5 seats: P₅ = 5! = 120.
  • Example 3 (course). 8 people in 8 seats: 8! = 40 320.
  • Example 4 (course). 2 red balls (R) and 3 white balls (B) in a row. n = 5, groups n₁ = 2, n₂ = 3: 5! / (2! × 3!) = 120 / 12 = 10.
  • Example 5 (course). Letters a, a, b: n = 3, groups 2 and 1: 3! / (2! × 1!) = 3: aab, aba, baa.
  • Example 6 (course). Words from the 7 letters of CELLULE: C ×1, E ×2, L ×3, U ×1. 7! / (1! 2! 3! 1!) = 5040 / 12 = 420.
  • Example 7 (course). Words from the 10 letters MISSISSIPI: M ×1, I ×4, S ×4, P ×1. 10! / (1! 4! 4! 1!) = 3 628 800 / 576 = 6300.

Combinations: pick p of n, order does not matter (combinaisons)

↑ contents
In plain words

Now you only care which objects you take, not in which order: a team, a hand of cards, a group of 3 numbers. {a, b} and {b, a} are the same group.

Count the ordered lists, then divide by p!, the number of ways to order each group: C(n, p) = A(n, p) / p!.

  • Combination (combinaison sans répétition)A group (subset) of p different objects taken from n. Count: C(n, p) = n! / (p! (n−p)!).C(5, 3) = 10
  • C(n, p) (C_n^p, “n choose p”)Read “n choose p”: the number of subsets of size p of a set of size n.
  • Combination with repetition (combinaison avec répétition)A group of p objects where the same object may be taken several times. Count: C(n+p−1, p) = (n+p−1)! / (p! (n−1)!).2 scoops from 3 flavours: C(4, 2) = 6
  • Pascal's triangle (triangle de Pascal)The table of all C(n, p), each number being the sum of the two above it.
  • Newton's binomial formula (binôme de Newton)(a + b)ⁿ = Σ C(n, k) a^k b^(n−k), k from 0 to n. The C(n, k) are the coefficients.(a+b)³ = a³ + 3a²b + 3ab² + b³
Worked example
  • Example 1 (course). E = {a, b, c}, p = 2: the groups are {a,b}, {a,c}, {b,c}, so C(3, 2) = 3 (compare with the 6 ordered pairs of section 2).
  • Example 2 (course). Groups of 3 from {1, 2, 3, 4, 5}: C(5, 3) = 5! / (3! × 2!) = 120 / (6 × 2) = 10, listed in the figure.
  • Properties (course). C(n, 0) = C(n, n) = 1 (one way to take nothing, one way to take everything). C(n, p) = C(n, n−p): choosing the p you keep is the same as choosing the n−p you leave. Example: C(5, 3) = C(5, 2) = 10.
  • Pascal's rule. C(n, p) = C(n−1, p−1) + C(n−1, p). Example: C(5, 3) = C(4, 2) + C(4, 3) = 6 + 4 = 10.
  • Newton's binomial. (a + b)³ = C(3,0)b³ + C(3,1)ab² + C(3,2)a²b + C(3,3)a³ = b³ + 3ab² + 3a²b + a³: the coefficients 1, 3, 3, 1 are row 3 of Pascal's triangle.
  • With repetition. Choose 2 ice-cream scoops among 3 flavours {a, b, c}, the same flavour allowed twice. n = 3, p = 2: C(3+2−1, 2) = C(4, 2) = 6: aa, ab, ac, bb, bc, cc.

Which formula? (récapitulatif)

↑ contents
In plain words

Before computing, answer three questions about the situation: does order matter? do I use all n objects or only p? can an object be used again? The answers point to exactly one formula.

  • Ordered list (suite ordonnée)Order matters: permutation (all n) or arrangement (p of n).
  • Unordered group (suite non ordonnée)Order does not matter: combination.
TypeOrder?Without repetitionWith repetition
Permutation (all n)yesn!n! / (n₁! … n_k!)
Arrangement (p of n)yesA(n, p) = n! / (n−p)!n^p
Combination (p of n)noC(n, p) = n! / (p! (n−p)!)C(n+p−1, p)
Worked example
  • Same data for all four: the letters {a, b, c, d} (n = 4), pick 2 (p = 2).
  • Order matters, no repetition (ab ≠ ba, no aa): A(4, 2) = 4 × 3 = 12.
  • Order matters, repetition allowed (aa allowed): 4² = 16.
  • Order does not matter, no repetition (ab = ba): C(4, 2) = 12 / 2! = 6.
  • Order does not matter, repetition allowed: C(4+2−1, 2) = C(5, 2) = 10.

Random experiment, Ω and events (expérience aléatoire, espace fondamental, évènement)

↑ contents
In plain words

A random experiment is something you can repeat in the same conditions but whose result you cannot know in advance: throwing a die.

Ω (omega) is the list of all possible results. An event is any group of results, described by a sentence that will be true or false after the experiment (“the die shows an even number”).

  • Random experiment E (expérience aléatoire, épreuve)An experiment whose result is decided by chance.throw a die
  • Sample space Ω (espace fondamental, univers)The set of all possible results. It can be finite, countably infinite or uncountable (see the examples).die: Ω = {1, …, 6}
  • Outcome ω (résultat élémentaire, réalisation)One single result, one element of Ω.ω = 4
  • Countable (dénombrable)Its elements can be listed one after the other and numbered 1, 2, 3, … (even if the list never ends).ℕ
  • Event A (évènement)A subset of Ω. A happens (is realised) if the result obtained is in A.“even” = {2, 4, 6}
  • Elementary event (évènement élémentaire)An event with a single outcome, written {ω}.{1}
  • Certain / impossible event (évènement certain / impossible)Ω always happens; the empty set ∅ never happens.
  • Inclusion A ⊂ B (inclusion)Every outcome of A is in B: if A happens, B happens too (A implies B).{6} ⊂ {2, 4, 6}
Worked example
  • Four sample spaces (course).
  • Throw a coin: Ω = {P, F}, |Ω| = 2 (finite).
  • Throw a die: Ω = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}, |Ω| = 6 (finite).
  • Throw a coin until the first P (heads): Ω = {P, FP, FFP, FFFP, …}: infinite but countable, you can number the results 1, 2, 3, …
  • Measure the lifetime of a light bulb: Ω = [0, +∞[: every positive real number, uncountable.
  • Events on the die (course). A “even” = {2, 4, 6}; B “odd” = {1, 3, 5}; C “multiple of 3” = {3, 6}; D “the smallest number” = {1}, an elementary event.

Combining events (opérations sur les évènements)

↑ contents
In plain words

Events are sets, so you combine them with set operations. “And” is the intersection ∩, “or” is the union ∪, “not” is the complement.

Two events are incompatible when they cannot happen together. A list of events that never overlap and together cover Ω is a complete system: exactly one of them happens.

  • A ∩ B (A and B) (intersection)Both A and B happen.
  • A ∪ B (A or B) (réunion, union)At least one of A, B happens.
  • Ā or A^c (not A) (complémentaire, contraire)A does not happen: all outcomes of Ω not in A.
  • A − B or A \ B (différence)A happens but B does not: A ∩ B̄.
  • A Δ B (différence symétrique)Exactly one of the two happens: (A − B) ∪ (B − A).
  • Incompatible (incompatibles, disjoints)A ∩ B = ∅: no common outcome.even and odd
  • Complete system (système complet d'évènements)A₁, …, A_n pairwise incompatible (A_i ∩ A_j = ∅ when i ≠ j) and A₁ ∪ … ∪ A_n = Ω. Also called a partition.{even, odd}
  • De Morgan's laws (lois de Morgan)not(A ∪ B) = Ā ∩ B̄ and not(A ∩ B) = Ā ∪ B̄. “Not (A or B)” means “neither A nor B”.
Worked example
  • Example 1 (course). Die, Ω = {1, …, 6}, A = even = {2, 4, 6}, B = odd = {1, 3, 5}, C = multiple of 3 = {3, 6}.
  • A ∩ B = ∅ (incompatible), A ∪ B = Ω, so {A, B} is a complete system.
  • A ∩ C = “even and multiple of 3” = {6}. A ∪ C = “even or multiple of 3” = {2, 3, 4, 6}.
  • Ā = “odd” = {1, 3, 5} = B. A − C = “even, not a multiple of 3” = {2, 4}. A Δ C = {2, 4} ∪ {3} = {2, 3, 4}.
  • Example 2 (course, exercise 2.1). Toss a coin 3 times and write the sequence. Ω = {PPP, PPF, PFP, PFF, FPP, FPF, FFP, FFF}, 2³ = 8 outcomes.
  • A = “at least two F” = {FFP, FPF, PFF, FFF}. B = “three F in a row or three P” = {FFF, PPP}.
  • A ∩ B = {FFF}. Ā = “at most one F” = {PPP, PPF, PFP, FPP}. A ∪ B = {FFP, FPF, PFF, FFF, PPP}.
  • Ā ∩ B̄ = not(A ∪ B) (De Morgan) = {PPF, PFP, FPP}.
  • Other rules (course). Order and brackets do not matter for ∪ alone or ∩ alone (associativity, commutativity). Distributivity: A ∩ (B ∪ C) = (A ∩ B) ∪ (A ∩ C) and A ∪ (B ∩ C) = (A ∪ B) ∩ (A ∪ C).

Algebra and σ-algebra (algèbre et tribu)

↑ contents
In plain words

Before giving probabilities, we fix which events are allowed to have one. That list must be “closed”: if A is in it, “not A” must be too, and so must “A or B”.

If the list is closed under finite unions it is an algebra; if it is also closed under infinite (countable) unions it is a σ-algebra (tribe). On a finite Ω both are the same thing.

  • Power set 𝒫(Ω) (ensemble des parties)The set of all subsets of Ω. If |Ω| = n, 𝒫(Ω) has 2ⁿ elements.Ω = {P, F}: {∅, {P}, {F}, Ω}
  • Algebra 𝒜 (algèbre)A family of subsets of Ω with (1) Ω ∈ 𝒜; (2) A ∈ 𝒜 ⇒ Ā ∈ 𝒜 (stable under complement); (3) A, B ∈ 𝒜 ⇒ A ∪ B ∈ 𝒜 (stable under finite union).
  • σ-algebra 𝒯 (tribu, σ-algèbre)Same, but (3) holds for any countable sequence A₁, A₂, …: their union is in 𝒯.
  • Consequences∅ is in it; it is stable under intersection (A ∩ B), difference and symmetric difference.
  • Classic σ-algebrasTrivial {∅, Ω}; full 𝒫(Ω); and {∅, A, Ā, Ω} for any event A.
  • Probabilisable space (Ω, 𝒯) (espace probabilisable)Ω together with a σ-algebra on it. On ℝ the usual σ-algebra is the Borel σ-algebra (tribu borélienne), built from the intervals.
Worked example
  • Power set (course). Ω = {1, 2, 3}, |Ω| = 3, so |𝒫(Ω)| = 2³ = 8: ∅, {1}, {2}, {3}, {1,2}, {1,3}, {2,3}, Ω.
  • Exercise (course). Ω = {1, 2, 3}, 𝒜₁ = {∅, {1}, {2,3}, Ω} and 𝒜₂ = {∅, {2}, {1,3}, Ω}.
  • 𝒜₁ contains Ω. Complements: ∅ ↔ Ω, {1} ↔ {2,3}, all inside. Unions: any union of these four is one of them. So 𝒜₁ is a σ-algebra; 𝒜₂ the same way.
  • 𝒜₁ ∩ 𝒜₂ = {∅, Ω}: the trivial σ-algebra, yes.
  • 𝒜₁ ∪ 𝒜₂ = {∅, {1}, {2}, {1,3}, {2,3}, Ω}: {1} ∪ {2} = {1, 2} is missing, so it is not a σ-algebra.
  • Example (course). On Ω = {w₁, w₂, w₃, w₄}, {∅, Ω, {w₁}, {w₂, w₃, w₄}} is an algebra: it is {∅, A, Ā, Ω} with A = {w₁}.

Probability and its rules (probabilité, axiomes de Kolmogorov)

↑ contents
In plain words

A probability P gives each allowed event a number between 0 and 1: its chance. Only two rules are imposed: the certain event Ω gets 1, and for events that cannot happen together, the chances add up.

Every other rule (complement, union, …) is a consequence. A good picture: a Venn diagram where each region holds a piece of probability and the whole box holds 1.

  • Probability P (probabilité)A function from the σ-algebra 𝒯 to [0, 1] with (1) P(Ω) = 1 and (2) σ-additivity.
  • σ-additivity (σ-additivité)For pairwise incompatible events E₁, E₂, …: P(E₁ ∪ E₂ ∪ …) = P(E₁) + P(E₂) + … (also for a finite list).
  • Probability space (Ω, 𝒯, P) (espace de probabilité, espace probabilisé)The triple: outcomes, allowed events, probability.
  • Poincaré formula (formule de Poincaré, inclusion-exclusion)P(A ∪ B ∪ C) = P(A) + P(B) + P(C) − P(A∩B) − P(A∩C) − P(B∩C) + P(A∩B∩C). Add singles, subtract pairs, add triples, …
Worked example
P(∅) = 0 P(Ā) = 1 − P(A) A ⊂ B ⇒ P(A) ≤ P(B) P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B) P(A − B) = P(A ∩ B̄) = P(A) − P(A ∩ B) P(A Δ B) = P(A) + P(B) − 2 P(A ∩ B) {A₁, …, A_n} complete system ⇒ P(B) = Σ P(B ∩ A_i)
  • Example (course). P(A) = 0.6, P(B) = 0.3, P(A ∩ B) = 0.15.
  • P(Ā) = 1 − 0.6 = 0.4.
  • P(A ∪ B) = 0.6 + 0.3 − 0.15 = 0.75 (the shared 0.15 was counted twice, so remove it once).
  • P(Ā ∩ B̄) = P(not(A ∪ B)) = 1 − 0.75 = 0.25 (De Morgan).
  • P(A \ B) = 0.6 − 0.15 = 0.45.
  • P(A Δ B) = 0.6 + 0.3 − 2 × 0.15 = 0.6 (= 0.45 + 0.15, the two “only” regions).
  • P(A Δ B̄) = P(A) + P(B̄) − 2 P(A ∩ B̄) = 0.6 + 0.7 − 2 × 0.45 = 0.4.
  • Poincaré, n = 3. Fair die, A = even = {2, 4, 6}, C = multiple of 3 = {3, 6}, L = “at most 3” = {1, 2, 3}. Pairs: A∩C = {6}, A∩L = {2}, C∩L = {3}; triple A∩C∩L = ∅.
  • P(A ∪ C ∪ L) = 3/6 + 2/6 + 3/6 − 1/6 − 1/6 − 1/6 + 0 = 5/6. Check: A ∪ C ∪ L = {1, 2, 3, 4, 6}, 5 faces.

Equally likely outcomes (équiprobabilité)

↑ contents
In plain words

When every outcome of a finite Ω has the same chance (fair die, well-mixed urn), the probability of an event is just a ratio of counts: favourable cases over possible cases.

This is where Part A is used: you count |A| and |Ω| with the counting formulas.

  • Equally likely (équiprobable, uniforme)All N outcomes have probability 1/N. (From σ-additivity: N × P({ω}) = 1.)fair die: 1/6 each
  • Favourable / possible (cas favorables / cas possibles)P(A) = |A| / |Ω|.
  • Draw at once (tirage simultané)Taking several balls together: order does not matter, use combinations.
Worked example
P(A) = |A| / |Ω| = number of favourable cases / number of possible cases
  • Example 1. Fair die, A = even = {2, 4, 6}: P(A) = 3/6 = 1/2.
  • Exercise 5.2 (course). An urn holds 16 white and 4 black balls, impossible to tell apart by touch. Draw 3 at once.
  • Possible cases: groups of 3 from 20 balls, C(20, 3) = 20 × 19 × 18 / 6 = 1140.
  • A = “2 white and 1 black”: choose 2 of the 16 white AND 1 of the 4 black: C(16, 2) × C(4, 1) = 120 × 4 = 480. P(A) = 480 / 1140 = 8/19 ≈ 0.421.
  • B = “at most 2 white” is the opposite of “3 white”. C(16, 3) = 560, so P(B) = 1 − 560/1140 = 580/1140 = 29/57 ≈ 0.509.
  • Exercise 5.1 (course): not equally likely. A loaded die: getting a prime number (2, 3, 5) is 3 times as likely as getting a non-prime (1, 4, 6). Probability of a number divisible by 3?
  • We assume faces inside each group are equally likely: call p the chance of one prime face and q of one non-prime face, with p = 3q.
  • Total = 1: 3p + 3q = 9q + 3q = 12q = 1, so q = 1/12 and p = 3/12 = 1/4.
  • Divisible by 3 = {3, 6}: one prime and one non-prime face, P = 1/4 + 1/12 = 1/3.

Conditional probability (probabilité conditionnelle)

↑ contents
In plain words

Sometimes you already know something happened: “the first toss was F”. The chance of A given B is computed inside the smaller world B: the part of A that lies in B, compared with all of B.

P(A | B) is itself a full probability (it gives 1 to Ω and adds up over incompatible events), just measured inside B.

  • P(A | B), “A given B” (probabilité de A sachant B, P(A/B))P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B), only when P(B) > 0.
  • Conditioning (conditionnement)Using the information “B happened” to update a chance.
Worked example
P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B) (P(B) > 0)
  • Exercise 6.1 (course). A fair coin is tossed twice. Probability that both tosses give F, knowing the first one is already F?
  • Ω = {PP, PF, FP, FF}, each 1/4. B = “first is F” = {FP, FF}, P(B) = 2/4 = 1/2. A = “both F” = {FF}, A ∩ B = {FF}, P = 1/4.
  • P(A | B) = (1/4) / (1/2) = 1/2. Without the information, P(A) = 1/4: knowing B doubled the chance.

Compound probability: a chain of steps (probabilités composées)

↑ contents
In plain words

Turn the definition around: the chance that A and B both happen = chance of the first × chance of the second given the first. For a chain of draws, multiply step by step, each time using what is left.

This is exactly how you read a probability tree: multiply along a branch.

  • Product rule (formule des probabilités composées)P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B | A) = P(B) × P(A | B).
  • Chain ruleP(A₁ ∩ A₂ ∩ … ∩ A_n) = P(A₁) × P(A₂ | A₁) × P(A₃ | A₁ ∩ A₂) × … × P(A_n | A₁ ∩ … ∩ A_{n−1}).
  • Without replacement (sans remise)A drawn ball is not put back, so the urn changes after each draw.
Worked example
P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B | A) P(A ∩ B ∩ C) = P(A) × P(B | A) × P(C | A ∩ B)
  • Exercise 6.2 (course). An urn holds 8 red and 4 white balls. Draw 2, one at a time, without putting back. Chance both are red?
  • R₁ = “1st red”: P(R₁) = 8/12. Given R₁, 7 red remain among 11: P(R₂ | R₁) = 7/11.
  • P(R₁ ∩ R₂) = 8/12 × 7/11 = 56/132 = 14/33 ≈ 0.424.
  • Check with counting (section 4): C(8, 2) / C(12, 2) = 28 / 66 = 14/33. Same answer.
  • Three draws. Same urn, 3 reds in a row: 8/12 × 7/11 × 6/10 = 14/55.

Total probability and Bayes (probabilités totales, formule de Bayes)

↑ contents
In plain words

Total probability: if the population splits into groups (a complete system B₁, …, B_n), the chance of A is the sum over the groups of “chance of the group × chance of A inside it”. On a tree: add the end of every branch that ends in A.

Bayes turns the question around. You know “A given each group”; you want “which group, given A”. Answer: the share of A that comes from that group.

  • Complete system B₁, …, B_n (système complet)Groups with no overlap that cover everything, each with P(B_i) > 0.men / women
  • Total probability (formule des probabilités totales)P(A) = Σ P(A | B_i) P(B_i). With two groups: P(A) = P(B) P(A | B) + P(B̄) P(A | B̄).
  • Bayes' formula (formule de Bayes)P(B_j | A) = P(A | B_j) P(B_j) / Σ P(A | B_i) P(B_i). Simple form: P(B | A) = P(A | B) P(B) / P(A).
Worked example
P(A) = Σ P(A | B_i) P(B_i) P(B_j | A) = P(A | B_j) P(B_j) / P(A)
  • Exercise (course, chapter 0). A population has 48% men (H) and so 52% women (F). 13% of men and 12% of women are diabetic (D). {H, F} is a complete system.
  • 1. A random person is diabetic? P(D) = P(D | H) P(H) + P(D | F) P(F) = 0.13 × 0.48 + 0.12 × 0.52 = 0.0624 + 0.0624 = 0.1248.
  • 2. Diabetic: chance it is a man? Bayes: P(H | D) = 0.0624 / 0.1248 = 0.5.
  • 3. Not diabetic: chance it is a woman? P(D̄) = 1 − 0.1248 = 0.8752. P(F ∩ D̄) = 0.52 × 0.88 = 0.4576. P(F | D̄) = 0.4576 / 0.8752 ≈ 0.523.
  • Exercise 6.3 (course), with numbers. Urn U₁ has a share p₁ of white balls, U₂ a share p₂. Pick an urn at random (1/2 each), draw a ball: it is white (W). Chance it came from U₁?
  • P(U₁ | W) = (½ p₁) / (½ p₁ + ½ p₂) = p₁ / (p₁ + p₂). With p₁ = 0.7 and p₂ = 0.2: 0.7 / 0.9 = 7/9 ≈ 0.778.
14

Independence (indépendance)

↑ contents
In plain words

Two events are independent when knowing that one happened does not change the chance of the other. Then the chance of both is simply the product.

Do not mix it up with incompatible: incompatible events block each other (if one happens, the other cannot), so they are strongly dependent.

  • Independent A, B (évènements indépendants)P(A | B) = P(A), or equivalently P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B).
  • ComplementsIf A, B are independent, so are (A, B̄), (Ā, B) and (Ā, B̄).
  • Extreme eventsIf P(A) = 0 or P(A) = 1, A is independent of every event.
  • Incompatible ≠ independentIf A ∩ B = ∅ with P(A), P(B) > 0, then P(A ∩ B) = 0 ≠ P(A)P(B): never independent.
  • Mutually independent (mutuellement (totalement) indépendants)A₁, A₂, A₃: every pair satisfies the product rule AND P(A₁ ∩ A₂ ∩ A₃) = P(A₁)P(A₂)P(A₃). For n events: the product rule for every sub-group of 2 or more.
Worked example
A, B independent ⇔ P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B)
  • Example 1. Fair die, A = even = {2, 4, 6}, C = multiple of 3 = {3, 6}. P(A) = 1/2, P(C) = 1/3, P(A ∩ C) = P({6}) = 1/6 = 1/2 × 1/3: independent.
  • Example 2. Same die, A = even and B = odd: P(A ∩ B) = 0 but P(A)P(B) = 1/4: incompatible, so not independent.
  • Example 3: pairs are not enough. Toss a fair coin twice. E₁ = “1st is P”, E₂ = “2nd is P”, E₃ = “both tosses the same”. Each has probability 1/2.
  • Every pair: E₁ ∩ E₂ = {PP}, E₁ ∩ E₃ = {PP}, E₂ ∩ E₃ = {PP}, each 1/4 = 1/2 × 1/2. So they are pairwise independent.
  • But E₁ ∩ E₂ ∩ E₃ = {PP} has probability 1/4, not 1/2 × 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/8: they are not mutually independent.
